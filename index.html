Product Smells

Clues That Your Product System Isn't Working as It Should

Jack Rust

Senior Product Manager, SEEK

Product Tank

01 · Saying the director's name too much

“The director doesn't like modals.”

Scenario: “The director doesn't like modals.” Also: “The director will like this.” / “The director won't like that.”

01 · Saying the director's name too much

  • BenignAligning to the vision and strategy
  • But…Their vision, not their opinions
  • SystemicA stage gate between you and the customer
  • Shows upProduct moves slowly, conservatively
  • What to doName it. “Could you explain why that is?”
Innocuous: Understanding leadership preferences and aligning to strategy. Why it’s suspicious: The thing that should matter is their vision (which they should be communicating), not their opinions. Disclaimer: The loop of putting work in front of them to test against their vision is fine. No one communicates in perfect fidelity, so guidance is still valuable. Systemic risk: Leadership has become a stage gate; work is optimised for approval rather than customer/business value. Their feedback becomes a kind of shadow strategy; those who can convince them gain a second role as the “director whisperer.” How this happens: Leader has strong opinions and a reputation for getting their way. Where this shows up: Things take longer than they need to and the company struggles to innovate — often a complaint of the same leader. This is a vicious feedback loop where the lack of “bottom-up” innovation forces this leader to do more of the leading. What to do: Name it; talk to the director if you can, otherwise your manager. “Hey, I heard you love X / hate Y — could you explain why that is, what you mean by that?” You might find they have no recollection of the advice.
Product Smells are a symptom of systemic shift

02 · Neat and tidy quarters

“We're booked up for Q2; maybe we can do it in Q3.”

Scenario: “We're booked up for Q2; maybe we can do it in Q3.”

02 · Neat and tidy quarters

  • BenignA useful shorthand for sequencing and capacity
  • But…Quarters aren’t real*
  • SystemicThe planning cycle has become a commitment
  • Shows upOpportunity cost
  • What to doChallenge the commitment
Innocuous: Quarters are a useful shorthand for sequencing, capacity and communicating plans. Why it’s suspicious: Quarters aren’t (usually) real — why do we treat them like they are anything more than a convenient cadence to realign? Disclaimer: In some industries quarters are real, because they are real to your customers. Systemic risk: The planning cycle has become a commitment; work is organised around preserving the quarter rather than continuously prioritising the most important thing. How this happens: Quarterly planning creates useful predictability, then gradually becomes promises, targets and expectations that are difficult to disrupt. Where this shows up: Important new work gets delayed because “it's not in the quarter”. Team members refuse to spend time on work that wasn’t planned for fear of the impact to their performance review. Teams protect plans even when they have learned something that should change them. What to do: Consider whether you could rearrange the quarter if something genuinely more important became known. If not, challenge the commitment mechanism — ideally by finding something more valuable.

03 · Engineering isn't arguing with you

Engineering isn't arguing with you

Scenario: Engineering readily agrees with the problem, proposed solution and priorities; most disagreement is about how to build it.

03 · Engineering isn't arguing with you

  • BenignStrong alignment. A shared understanding.
  • But…What are the chances everyone agrees?
  • SystemicEngineering doesn't feel able to challenge.
  • Shows upRework. Surprises in UAT. Fixing things after launch.
  • What to doGive them ammunition to argue with.
Innocuous: Strong product/engineering alignment and a shared understanding of the work. Why it’s suspicious: What are the chances that everyone consistently agrees with the PM's understanding of what and why? Systemic risk: Engineering doesn't feel able to challenge product thinking, or their incentives primarily reward shipping rather than shaping what should be built. How this happens: Engineers don't feel they can win the argument, product and engineering are overly separated, or engineering is primarily incentivised around delivery. Where this shows up: Rework later, surprises in UAT, engineering concerns appearing after build rather than during discovery, and the PM spending lots of effort fixing things after launch. What to do: Give engineers something worthwhile to argue about. Invite them into the “why”, not just the “how”. Continuous discovery can make this a shared activity rather than a handoff.

04 · Hoping for nods

Aiming for a thumbs up from leadership

Scenario: Before a leadership meeting, you collect everyone's views and construct a position that you expect everyone to agree with.

04 · Hoping for nods

  • BenignPre-alignment prevents surprises
  • But…Aligned or uncontroversial
  • SystemicMeetings have become approval rituals
  • Shows upDecisions remain vague
  • What to doAim to test for misalignment
Innocuous: Pre-alignment prevents surprises and makes meetings efficient. Why it’s suspicious: If all disagreement has been removed before the meeting, there may be little left for the meeting to accomplish. Disclaimer: Pre-alignment is good. The smell is when its purpose becomes eliminating disagreement rather than making the eventual discussion better. Systemic risk: Meetings have become approval rituals rather than places to surface disagreement, test thinking and make decisions. How this happens: Disagreement is uncomfortable, politically costly or interpreted as poor preparation. A “good meeting” becomes one where everyone leaves agreeing. Where this shows up: Meetings go very smoothly but decisions remain vague; people nod along without genuinely buying in; disagreements emerge afterwards in private conversations. What to do: Pre-align on context and known constraints, but deliberately bring the unresolved question into the room. Put forward a view that people can meaningfully agree or disagree with.

05 · “We'll learn from the data”

“Let's run an experiment and see what happens.”

Scenario: “Let's run an experiment and see what happens.” Also: “Let's launch it and see what the data tells us.”

05 · “We'll learn from the data”

  • BenignExperiments generate evidence
  • But…An experiment should help you make a decision
  • SystemicExperiments as research rather than validation
  • Shows upProduct is slow. Experiments are wasteful.
  • What to doWork backwards from the decision
Innocuous: Experiments generate evidence and reduce uncertainty. Why it’s suspicious: An experiment should help you make a decision, not postpone making one. Disclaimer: Exploratory experiments won't always have a predetermined decision. But where the experiment is intended to resolve a known decision, the decision can usually be defined first. Systemic risk: The organisation doesn't reward decisions under uncertainty. Evidence becomes a socially acceptable way to defer commitment and accountability. How this happens: Being wrong is more visible than being indecisive. “Let's get more data” is a safer position than making a call that can later be judged. Where this shows up: Any result generates another round of analysis; thresholds move after results are known; teams discover reasons to continue regardless of the outcome; decisions repeatedly wait for “one more piece of evidence.” What to do: Ask, “Could we have made this decision before seeing the data?” Where possible, pre-commit: “If X is above this threshold, we'll do A; if it's below, we'll do B.” The experiment provides the variable; you decide what you'll do with it.

  • Be suspicious
  • Smells can be a sign of system drift
  • Cause and effect show up in different places
  • Shine a light on the shadow system
The best way to test the shadow system is to shine a light on it.

06 · Getting work ready for engineering

“I need to get something ready for engineering.”

“I don't want engineering sitting on their hands.”

Scenario: “I need to get something ready for engineering.” / “I don't want engineering sitting on their hands.”

06 · Getting work ready for engineering

  • BenignMaking good use of the people available
  • But…Engineers don't need a constant supply of product work
  • SystemicPulled forward to utilise engineering capacity
  • Shows upHalf-baked ideas get accelerated
  • What to doAsk whether the work is important independent of engineering capacity
Innocuous: Making good use of the people available and keeping work flowing. Why it’s suspicious: Engineers don't need a constant supply of product work to be useful; there is always valuable engineering work that doesn't require a PM to prepare it. Systemic risk: Product work is being pulled forward to utilise engineering capacity rather than because it is the most important thing to do. How this happens: Engineering capacity is visible and expensive; idle engineers feel like waste. The team implicitly treats engineers as needing a continuous supply of feature work. Where this shows up: Half-baked ideas get accelerated, discovery gets compressed, and low-value work gets prioritised because “we've got the engineers.” Technical work that would otherwise be valuable can become harder to justify. What to do: Ask whether the work is important independent of engineering capacity. If there isn't enough valuable work for the team, treat that as information about team shape or staffing rather than a reason to manufacture work.

07 · Unclenching when it's delivered

You feel like the work is finally over.

Scenario: The thing launches, the metrics look good, everyone celebrates — and you feel like the work is finally over.

07 · Unclenching when it's delivered

  • BenignReaching a milestone and taking a well-earned break
  • But…Not necessarily the end of the work
  • Systemic“Done” means delivering the thing
  • Shows upInitiatives are closed as soon as they launch
  • What to doJump off because something else is more valuable
Innocuous: Reaching a milestone, delivering something valuable and taking a well-earned break. Why it’s suspicious: Product work rarely has a natural point at which the problem becomes “done.” The release is an event, not necessarily the end of the work. Disclaimer: Celebrate the release. Celebrate constantly. Product work is hard and successful delivery is worth recognising. Systemic risk: Product work has acquired a definition of “done” based on delivering the thing rather than improving the product. The release, first-month metrics or movement in a metric becomes the finish line. How this happens: Delivery has a concrete endpoint, ceremony and reward. Continuous product improvement is less tangible and has no natural completion point. Where this shows up: Initiatives are closed as soon as they launch; diminishing returns are interpreted as completion rather than a reason to move to the next most valuable problem; teams celebrate shipping more than learning or improving. What to do: Celebrate the release, then ask what the next highest-value improvement is. Jump off because something else is more valuable, not simply because this thing shipped.
